## The same loop, on a bigger tree

You have watched CFR solve a three-card game. A Hold'em solver runs the same loop. The difference is the size of what it runs on, and the shortcuts needed to make that size manageable.

## The game tree

A **game tree** is every sequence of events a hand can follow. Each **node** is a moment where a player acts or a card is dealt. The branches are the options: check, each allowed bet size, fold, call, each allowed raise.

At a decision node the solver stores, for every hand the acting player could hold, a probability for each action. Solving means filling in all of those numbers so that neither player can improve.

The tree for real no-limit Hold'em is beyond any computer. From one flop there are {int(runouts)} turn and river runouts. There are {int(flops)} flops. At every node a player may bet any amount from one blind to a full stack. So solvers do not solve poker. They solve a model of it.

## Abstraction

**Abstraction** means replacing the game with a smaller one that keeps what matters.

**Betting abstraction.** You choose the allowed sizes at each node, such as a third of the pot, three quarters, and all-in. The solver cannot use a size you did not give it. This is the biggest simplification and the one you control.

**Scope.** Most study is done on **one flop at a time**, starting from the flop with preflop ranges supplied by you. Preflop is solved separately, with its own simplifications.

**Card abstraction.** Larger solves group similar hands or similar runouts together. Suit isomorphism is the harmless version: boards that differ only by renaming suits are the same problem.

Each simplification has a price. A tree with only small bets cannot discover that an overbet is best. Ranges that are too tight or too wide give an answer for a spot that does not occur. The output is exactly as good as the model.

## One iteration

Each pass of CFR does what you saw in Kuhn poker, at every node of the tree.

1. Walk the tree with the current strategies.
2. At each decision, for each hand, compute the value of each action and how it compares with the current mix.
3. Add the differences to the running regrets.
4. Set the next strategy from positive regrets.
5. Keep the average.

Thousands of iterations later, the average strategy is close to an equilibrium of the model.

## Exploitability, again

A solver stops when exploitability drops below a target, usually given as a percentage of the pot. Read that number with two cautions.

- It is measured **inside the model**. The best response is limited to the same bet sizes. Low exploitability does not mean a human with other sizes cannot do better.
- It is an average over the whole tree. Rare lines deep in the tree are solved less accurately than common ones, since they are visited with little weight.

## What the output is

A solve gives you, at every node, a strategy grid and an EV for each hand. It is tempting to treat this as an answer key. Three reasons not to:

1. **You will not face that exact spot again.** Change the stack by ten blinds or one card in a range and the frequencies shift.
2. **Your opponents do not play the other side of it.** The solver's strategy is a best response to a perfect opponent. Against a real one, other plays earn more.
3. **Nobody can memorize it.** A single flop has thousands of nodes.

## How to study with one

Use the solver to learn why, then carry the why.

- **Ask aggregate questions first.** How often does each player bet on this flop, and at what size? Which player has the range and nut advantage? This connects to Module 4.
- **Compare boards.** Run several flops of one type and several of another. The pattern across boards is the lesson.
- **Look at which hands do what.** What do the bluffs have in common? Usually blockers and backdoor equity. What do the checked strong hands have in common?
- **Change one input and re-solve.** Remove a bet size, widen a range, shorten the stacks. What moves tells you what the strategy depended on.
- **Check the EV difference.** If two actions differ by a hundredth of a big blind, the choice is a detail. If they differ by a big blind, it is a principle.
- **Then lock a leak.** The next lesson covers forcing one player to make a known mistake and seeing how the other should respond.

The tools on this site are deliberately small: a toy river game, Kuhn poker, heads-up push or fold, and exact range equities. They are the pieces a full solver is made of, and they are small enough that you can check every number.

<Remember>

- A solver finds an approximate equilibrium of a simplified game tree
- You choose the abstraction: bet sizes, ranges, stacks. The output answers that model only
- CFR adjusts toward regretted actions and reports the average strategy
- Exploitability is measured inside the model
- Study patterns, comparisons and reasons, not frequencies
- Change one input at a time to see what the strategy depends on

</Remember>

<Mistakes>

- Treating solver output as the answer to real poker
- Feeding in unrealistic ranges and trusting the result
- Memorizing grids for one board
- Copying a balanced strategy against an opponent with large, obvious leaks

</Mistakes>
