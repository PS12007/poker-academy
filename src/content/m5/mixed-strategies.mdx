<Plain>Solvers sometimes play the same hand two ways. That only happens when both ways are worth the same. Against real opponents, pick the better one, and do not worry about exact percentages.</Plain>

## Same hand, same spot, different actions

A **pure** strategy always does the same thing with a given hand in a given situation. A **mixed** strategy randomizes: this hand bets 30% of the time and checks 70%.

Solver outputs are full of mixes, and they put people off. A grid where half the cells are split two or three ways looks impossible to play. It helps to understand why the mixes are there, because that tells you which ones matter.

## Why mix at all

You saw it in the toy game. If the air always bluffs, the caller always calls. If it never bluffs, the caller always folds. No pure strategy survives being known. The only stable answer is a frequency, set so that the opponent gains nothing by adjusting.

The mix has one job: to control the opponent's incentives. It is about the range, not the hand. In the toy game the bettor needed a certain number of bluffs in total. Which particular air hands supplied them did not matter.

## Which hands mix

A hand is mixed only when it is **indifferent**: both actions have the same EV. That is a narrow condition, so in any spot most hands are pure.

- Hands clearly good enough always take the aggressive action.
- Hands clearly too weak never do.
- A band of hands at the boundary is close to indifferent and makes up the difference.

Out of 169 hands, only a few carry a number. Everything else is always or never. Move the stack by half a big blind and the mixed hands change, because the boundary has moved.

In bigger games there is a second source of mixing. A hand can be indifferent between two bet sizes, or between betting and checking, because the range needs some of each. Strong hands are split between betting and checking so that the checking range is not defenseless.

## How much the frequencies matter

Here is the part that makes mixed strategies livable.

**Against an equilibrium opponent, the choice does not affect the hand's EV.** That is what indifferent means. If the solver calls 60% and folds 40%, then calling always, folding always and anything between all earn the same with that hand.

So where is the cost of getting it wrong? It appears only if the opponent notices and adjusts. If you never bluff in a spot where the equilibrium bluffs a third of the time, a perfect opponent folds every bluff catcher and your value bets earn less. Against an opponent who is not adjusting, the cost is zero.

And if the opponent is not at equilibrium, the hand is usually not indifferent at all. One action is strictly better.

Away from the 50% line, the mix is worse than choosing the better action every time. Mixing is the right answer to an opponent who plays perfectly, and a hedge when you do not know which way they lean.

## Making it playable

Nobody rolls dice for every decision. Three practical tools:

**Round to pure.** If a hand is 85% bet and 15% check, bet it. The loss is negligible against anyone, and you remove a decision.

**Split by a property of the hand.** To bluff half of some hand class, bluff the combos with one feature and give up with the others. Choose the feature so the bluffing half has the better blockers. Many players split by suit: this pair of suits bets, that pair checks.

**Randomize with something neutral** when it matters, such as the second hand on a clock or the suit of a specific card. This is for close, high-stakes spots against observant opponents.

Most important is to carry the reason rather than the number. Remembering that a spot needs some bluffs with good blockers and some strong hands in the checking range is worth more than memorizing 37%.

<Remember>

- A mixed strategy plays one hand more than one way at set frequencies
- Mixing exists to keep the opponent indifferent. It is a property of the range
- Only indifferent hands mix. Most hands are pure
- Against equilibrium, any frequency with an indifferent hand earns the same
- Against a leak, pick the better action every time
- Simplify by rounding, or by splitting combos on suits and blockers

</Remember>

<Mistakes>

- Trying to memorize frequencies to the percent
- Mixing against an opponent with an obvious leak
- Believing a 60/40 mix means the 60% action is better
- Using a visible or patterned way to randomize

</Mistakes>
