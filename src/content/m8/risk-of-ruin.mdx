## The formula

Model the bankroll as a random walk that drifts upward at the winrate and wobbles by the standard deviation. The chance that such a walk, starting at B, ever touches zero is

<Formula name="risk of ruin">RoR = exp(−2 × winrate × B / sd²)</Formula>

with winrate and standard deviation per 100 hands and B in big blinds. It assumes a fixed stake forever, a constant winrate, and normally distributed results over blocks of hands. None is exactly true, but the formula captures the shape of the risk well.

## How it scales

Because the bankroll is in the exponent, adding bankroll cuts risk fast. Every additional amount sd² / (2 × winrate) multiplies the risk by about 0.37.

Doubling the bankroll squares the risk. A 20% risk becomes 4%, and 4% becomes 0.16%.

Winrate works the same way, which is uncomfortable: if your true winrate is half what you believe, your real risk is the square root of the risk you calculated. A comfortable 1% becomes 10%.

## Zero edge means certain ruin

Set the winrate to zero and the exponent is zero: RoR = 1. A walk with no drift wanders without limit in both directions and, given enough time, reaches any level below it. A losing player is in the same position, only faster. No bankroll is big enough to protect a player without an edge, and this includes a player whose edge is erased by rake.

## Kelly-style thinking

The formula assumes you never change stakes. Real players should, and a principle from betting theory explains why.

The **Kelly criterion** says that to maximize long-run growth you should risk a fixed **fraction** of your current bankroll, not a fixed amount. After losses you bet less, after wins more. A bettor who follows it, in idealized conditions, can never reach exactly zero.

Poker players cannot choose any stake they like, but moving down a level when the bankroll falls below a threshold does the same job. It converts a fixed-amount risk into something close to a fractional one. That is why a sensible move-down rule makes true ruin rare even with a modest bankroll. The cost is time spent at lower stakes, which is cheap compared with going broke.

Full Kelly is aggressive and assumes you know your edge exactly. Most people who use Kelly-style sizing use a fraction of it, half or less, to allow for an uncertain winrate.
