## Tight and aggressive

A complete beginner strategy fits in one sentence: **play few hands, and bet them hard.**

**Tight** is about selection. Most starting hands lose money on average, because they make weak pairs and second-best hands. Throwing them away costs you nothing but patience.

**Aggressive** is about how you play the hands you keep. Betting and raising gives you two ways to win: your opponent can fold, or you can have the best hand. Checking and calling gives you one. Players who mostly call need the best hand every time.

The combination works against weak opposition for a plain reason. You show up with stronger hands than they do, and you make them pay to see whether theirs is good enough.

How to read the grid: pairs run down the diagonal. Hands above the diagonal are **suited** (both cards the same suit, written with an s). Hands below are **offsuit** (written with an o). Suited hands are a little stronger because they can make flushes, but only a little. Being suited does not rescue a bad hand.

## Why the weak hands lose

The hands that get beginners into trouble are the ones that look playable: an ace or a king with a small side card. The problem is **domination**. When K-9 makes a pair of kings, the hands that give it real action usually hold a king with a better kicker.

The engine can put a number on it. Ace-jack against ace-king has only <Eq a="AhJc" b="AsKd" /> equity. That is less than seven-deuce has against the same ace-king (<Eq a="7h2c" b="AsKd" />), because seven-deuce at least has two cards of its own to pair. King-nine against king-queen has <Eq a="Kh9c" b="KsQd" />. In both cases you are behind and you will not find out until you have paid for the lesson.

## How to enter the pot

**If nobody has raised: raise or fold.** Do not just call the big blind. A raise to about 2.5 to 3 big blinds is standard. Add one big blind for each player who has already called.

Raising does three things calling cannot. It can win the blinds on the spot. It thins the field, which makes your strong hands more likely to hold up. And it gives you the lead in the betting after the flop.

**If someone has raised before you:** tighten up sharply. With the very best hands (the top pairs and ace-king) raise again, to about three times their raise. Fold nearly everything else for now. Module 3 fills in the rest.

## After the flop

An unpaired hand flops a pair or better only {pct(probFlopPairOrBetterUnpaired())} of the time. Your opponents miss just as often, which gives the preflop raiser a simple plan.

1. **You raised and hit top pair or better.** Bet. Aim for about half to two thirds of the pot. You want worse pairs and draws to call.
2. **You raised and missed, against one opponent.** A small bet, about a third of the pot, will often win it. If it is called and you do not improve, slow down.
3. **You raised and missed, against two or more.** Mostly check. Someone usually has something.
4. **You face a large bet or a raise and hold one pair.** Be willing to fold. Large bets and raises from typical opponents are value far more often than bluffs.

None of this is optimal. It is a set of defaults that keeps you out of expensive situations while you learn the reasons, and the reasons are what the rest of the course is for.

<Mistakes>

- Playing any two suited cards. Suitedness adds only a few points of equity
- Calling the big blind instead of raising
- Refusing to fold top pair to heavy action
- Getting bored and widening the chart. Folding is most of the job

</Mistakes>
