## Hands are not equally likely

There are {int(facts.totalCombos)} different two-card holdings in a 52-card deck. Many are the same hand for practical purposes: the ace of spades with the king of spades plays the same before the flop as the ace of hearts with the king of hearts. Grouping those together leaves {facts.distinctHands} distinct starting hands.

Those {facts.distinctHands} are not equally likely, and that fact is the foundation of hand reading. A specific two-card holding is called a **combination**, or combo.

| Hand type | Example | Combos | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pocket pair | Q-Q | 6 | Choose 2 of the 4 suits |
| Suited | A-K suited | 4 | One per suit |
| Offsuit | A-K offsuit | 12 | 4 × 3 |
| Unpaired, any suits | A-K | 16 | 4 × 4 |

The 13 pairs, 78 suited hands and 78 offsuit hands add up correctly: 13 × 6 + 78 × 4 + 78 × 12 = {int(facts.totalCombos)}.

Two consequences follow at once. You are dealt a pocket pair {pct(facts.pPair)} of the time, which is 1 hand in 17. You are dealt aces {pct(facts.pAces, 2)} of the time, 1 hand in 221. And when an opponent raises, ace-king is far more likely than aces: 16 combos against 6.

## Card removal

Every card you can see is a card your opponent does not hold. Counting combos after removing the visible cards is called **card removal**, and a card that removes combos from an opponent's range is a **blocker**.

Suppose you hold the ace of spades and the king of diamonds.

- Pocket aces: three aces remain, so 3 combos instead of 6.
- Pocket kings: 3 combos instead of 6.
- Ace-king: three aces times three kings is 9 combos instead of 16.
- Pocket queens: untouched, still 6.

The board removes cards the same way. On a king-high flop, one king is gone, and there are only 3 combos of pocket kings. This is why sets are always a small part of anyone's range.

## Counting a range

A **range** is the full set of hands a player can hold in a situation. Counting its combos tells you how its parts compare.

Say an opponent only re-raises all-in with queens or better and ace-king. That range has {full} combos: 18 pairs and 16 ace-kings. So nearly half of it is ace-king, a hand that has not made a pair yet.

Now give yourself ace-king. The range shrinks to {blocked} combos: queens 6, kings 3, aces 3, ace-king 9. If instead you held queens, you would face 1 combo of queens, 6 kings, 6 aces and 16 ace-kings.

<Example title="Value or bluff on the river">

The board is K-9-4-7-2 with no flush possible. Your opponent bets, and you believe their value hands are sets and top pair with a good kicker: K-K, 9-9, 4-4, A-K and K-Q.

Count them with the board removed. Each set has 3 combos, so the sets total {sets}. A-K and K-Q each have 3 kings times 4 side cards, so 12 each, {topPair} together. Their value range is {sets + topPair} combos, and {pct(topPair / (sets + topPair), 0)} of it is one pair.

If you hold two pair, you beat {topPair} of those {sets + topPair} combos (a few less of each once your own two cards are removed). Counting turned a vague fear of a set into a number: sets are {pct(sets / (sets + topPair), 0)} of the hands they bet for value.

</Example>

## Why blockers matter

Blockers shift the odds a little before the flop and a great deal on later streets, when ranges are narrow and a few combos are a large share of them.

- **Calling.** If you hold a card that blocks their value hands, more of their betting range is bluffs.
- **Bluffing.** If you hold a card that blocks the hands they would call with, your bluff works more often. Holding the ace of a suit on a three-flush board is the classic case: they cannot have the nut flush.
- **Unblocking.** The reverse also matters. You want not to hold the cards that make up their folding range.

<Mistakes>

- Treating every hand in a range as equally likely
- Fearing sets and pocket aces out of proportion to their combo counts
- Forgetting to remove board cards and your own cards before counting
- Ignoring that unpaired hands outnumber pairs

</Mistakes>
