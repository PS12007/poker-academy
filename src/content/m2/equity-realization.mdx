## Equity assumes a free ride

**Equity** is your share of the pot if all the remaining cards are dealt and nobody bets again. Calculators report it, and pot odds are compared against it. But in a real hand there are three more rounds of betting, and betting changes who gets to showdown.

If you have 30% equity and your opponent bets on every street, you will fold on some of them. The hands you fold never get to collect their 30%. On the other side, if you are the one betting and they fold a hand that had 30%, you just collected a share that was not yours.

**Equity realization** is the fraction of raw equity a hand captures once that betting is accounted for.

<Formula name="realization">realized share of pot = raw equity × realization factor</Formula>

A factor of 1 means you capture your equity exactly. Below 1 you **under-realize**. Above 1 you **over-realize**. The factor is not a constant you can look up. It depends on the hand, the positions, the stacks and the players. Solvers compute it as a byproduct. At the table you estimate its direction.

## What moves the factor

**Position.** The player who acts last can check behind to see a free card, bet when checked to, and call knowing the price. In-position hands commonly capture more than their raw share and out-of-position hands less. This is the single largest effect.

**Playability.** Hands that make clear strong hands or clear draws realize well, because they can continue against bets for good reason. Hands that make weak pairs realize badly: they are ahead often enough to have equity and behind often enough that they cannot call three streets.

**Suitedness and connectedness.** A suited hand has only a few points more raw equity than its offsuit twin. Its real advantage is that it flops flush draws and backdoor draws, which give it profitable reasons to keep betting or calling. Connected cards do the same with straights.

**Having the initiative.** The player who raised last can win by betting when both players miss. The caller has to make a hand or make a stand.

**Stack depth.** Deep stacks reward hands that can make the nuts. Short stacks reward raw equity, because once the money is all-in there is no more betting and every hand realizes exactly 100%.

## Raw equity can mislead

By this measure A-2 offsuit ({pct(ev('A2o'))}) is well ahead of 7-6 suited ({pct(ev('76s'))}). Yet strong players open 7-6 suited from more seats than A-2 offsuit, and call raises with it far more readily.

The reason is realization. A-2 offsuit gets its equity from winning unimproved with ace high and from pairing the ace with no kicker. Neither can call a big bet comfortably. When it puts a lot of money in, it is usually behind. 7-6 suited gets its equity from straights, flushes, two pair and strong draws. Those are hands that can put money in confidently, or draws with a known price. One hand leaks equity on every street and the other keeps it.

The all-in number is still the right one when the hand will be all-in. With ten big blinds in a tournament, A-2 offsuit is the better hand to shove. Context decides which measure applies.

<Remember>

- Raw equity assumes no more betting. Real hands have betting
- Realization is the share of raw equity a hand actually captures
- In position, suited, connected, and with the betting lead: realize more
- Out of position with weak pairs and no draws: realize less
- All-in, every hand realizes exactly its equity

</Remember>

<Mistakes>

- Choosing hands to play by raw equity alone
- Calling out of position with hands that cannot continue on most flops
- Overvaluing weak offsuit aces and kings because they are ahead of a random hand
- Undervaluing position when comparing two close options

</Mistakes>
